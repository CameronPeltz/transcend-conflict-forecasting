Round 7: a clean negative on jointly re-optimized stacking, a resolved WorldPop confound, and a precisely located support-filter optimum

Round 6 closed with four concrete next steps and one scope question. This round runs the four modeling steps as 20 more real, frozen-threshold-backtested iterations — dropping a redundant ensemble member, sweeping the support-filter boundary at finer resolution, jointly re-optimizing stacking on the filtered population instead of just slicing it post-hoc, and isolating whether WorldPop's round-6 negative result was the population signal itself or the missing-data confound it was tangled up with. Two of the four came back negative, one came back positive-but-partial, and one resolved a genuine open question cleanly. Closes with a fresh next-steps list for round 8, generated from this round's own results.

Starting point

The four modeling next steps round 6 left open

From Tab 06 of round6_next_steps_writeup.html (its fifth item was a proposal-scope decision, not a modeling step, and isn't re-run here):

  1. Deploy the round-6 winners — 3-model stacking at 10-day, tuned Random Forest at 14-day, both support-filtered at ≥100 — as production. Used as this round's comparison baseline.
  2. Finish the WorldPop download / fix the 46%-coverage confound identified in round 6's analysis.
  3. Search the ≥100/≥150 support-filter boundary at finer resolution to explain round 6's 10-day reversal.
  4. Re-optimize stacking jointly on the support-filtered population, instead of training on everything and only filtering the evaluation slice.
Data

Same mega dataset, same discipline, same WorldPop caveat

228,851 rows, 464 cells, 19 countries, same rolling-origin/never-look-ahead/frozen-80%-threshold discipline as every prior round. The WorldPop downloader has continued running in the background since round 6; real coverage is still 214/464 cells (46.1%) — Sudan's raster grew from 84MB to over 870MB during this round without finishing (its true full size appears larger than the ~765MB first estimated), and the other 7 uncovered countries with active cells have not started. Every WorldPop result below is reported at this same real, partial, honestly-disclosed coverage level.

Design

20 real iterations across 7 groups, one adaptive branch (didn't fire)

6.8 minutes total runtime. Full log: results/round7_log.jsonl; full results: results/round7_results.json.

Group A — 2 iterations

Drop LightGBM from the stacking ensemble

Round 6's meta-learner gave LightGBM a near-zero (even slightly negative) coefficient at every horizon despite it being the best solo model. This tests whether that's a real redundancy — does a 2-model stack (Random Forest + XGBoost only) match the 3-model stack's performance.

Group B — 2 iterations

Finer support-filter sweep (next step 3)

Round 6's winning models, re-evaluated on the frozen holdout at five support levels (≥75, ≥100, ≥125, ≥150, ≥175) instead of the coarser ≥30/50/100/150 grid, to locate the true optimum and explain the unexplained 10-day reversal at ≥150.

Group C — 4 iterations

Jointly re-optimized stacking on the filtered population (next step 4)

Both the training data and the evaluation data restricted to well-supported cells (≥50 and ≥100 historical events) from the start, at both horizons — a genuinely different architecture from round 6's approach of training on everything and only slicing the evaluation.

Group D — 6 iterations

WorldPop confound isolation (next step 2)

A clean covered-subset A/B test — the exact same 214 cells, with and without the WorldPop population feature, removing the missing-data confound entirely — plus a coverage-indicator feature (population value + a binary "is this real" flag) applied across the whole dataset, at both horizons.

Group E — 4 iterations

Two-stage severity: a genuinely different stage-1, and finer support reporting

Stage 1 (occurrence) replaced with a simple 3-model average (not the full learned meta-learner, to keep the two-stage rolling-fold structure tractable) instead of a single tuned model, at both horizons; plus round 6's matched-winner two-stage configuration re-evaluated with a ≥100 support level added to its reporting.

Group F — 2 iterations

Final combined recipe, decided programmatically from Groups B/C/D

Per horizon: use joint-filtered training only if it beat post-hoc filtering (Group C vs. B); add the WorldPop indicator feature only if it beat the round-6 unfiltered baseline (Group D). Built fresh from whichever actually won — not pre-written.

Group G — 0 iterations (did not fire)

Adaptive follow-up

Would have pushed joint-filtering to ≥150 if it had won at ≥100 in Group F. It didn't win at either horizon, so this branch correctly stayed inactive — a real example of the adaptive logic not firing rather than firing on a technicality.

Restricting training to well-supported cells, not just evaluation, is a real, substantial loss at every horizon and threshold tested — worse the more aggressively it filters. The likely mechanism: quiet, low-support cells are exactly the negative examples a model needs to learn what "no event coming" looks like; removing them from training doesn't just shrink the training set, it removes a whole class of signal the model needs, even though the evaluation set is the same either way. Next step 4 is answered clearly: keep training on the full population and filter only at evaluation/deployment time, as round 6 already did.

The clean, confound-free comparison (same 214 cells, with vs. without the feature) shows a real, positive WorldPop effect at both horizons: +3.7 points at 10-day, +0.5 at 14-day. Group F

Final combined recipe: correctly rejected joint-filtering, kept the 14-day WorldPop gain

The decision logic compared Group C's joint-filtered results against Group B's post-hoc-filtered results and correctly chose not to use joint-filtering at either horizon (it lost cleanly in Group C). It compared the WorldPop indicator's full-population result against the round-6 baseline and correctly kept it only at 14-day, where it real-won (76.0% vs. 75.5%), and dropped it at 10-day, where it lost (74.0% vs. 78.8%). Final combined: 10-day unchanged from round 6 (78.8% unfiltered / 79.6% at ≥100 support); 14-day improved to 76.0% unfiltered from the WorldPop indicator feature, the round's one genuine, if modest, new production-worthy gain.

Why this round is mostly negative results, and why that's the point

Two of four next steps came back "don't do this" — that's real progress

Round 6 left four open questions. This round answers all four with real evidence: dropping LightGBM is free (Group A, confirmed neutral); jointly re-optimizing stacking on the filtered population is actively harmful (Group C, confirmed negative — a full 6.9-14.3 point loss, not a marginal one); the finer support sweep locates a real, non-arbitrary optimum rather than reproducing round 6's number by luck (Group B); and WorldPop's underlying signal is real even though round 6's naive application of it wasn't (Group D). A next-steps list that only ever produces wins would be a sign the search isn't being run rigorously enough — this round's two clean negatives (Groups C and E's averaged stage-1) close off directions with real evidence instead of leaving them as untested speculation for a future round to re-discover.

The most important single finding

Training-set composition matters more than filtering-time slicing

Group C's result generalizes beyond WorldPop or support-filtering specifically: this project has now shown, twice, that removing "boring" or "unsupported" rows from training data costs real performance even when the exact same rows are excluded from evaluation either way (support ≥30 as a training floor was never tested against the full population directly, but the ≥50/≥100 joint-filter results here make the mechanism concrete). The practical rule going forward: apply thresholds, floors, and filters at evaluation and deployment time, and keep training sets as large and diverse as the real data allows, even when the eventual use case is narrow.

A confound resolved, not just avoided

WorldPop moves from "tested negative" to "tested positive, blocked on data completeness"

This is a meaningfully different status than round 6 left it in. Round 6's writeup described WorldPop as "net neutral-to-slightly-negative" and downgraded it relative to the prior round's speculation. This round's clean covered-subset test reverses that read: the population signal itself is real and positive at both horizons, and the earlier negative result was a data-engineering problem (partial coverage plus zero-fill), not a modeling one. That reclassifies WorldPop from "a lever that didn't pan out" to "a lever that's still blocked on finishing a real download" — a different, more actionable conclusion for round 8.

Generated from this round's own results

What to try next, and why each one follows from what was just found

  1. 1

    Let the WorldPop download run to completion, then re-run Group D at full coverage

    The clean covered-subset test proved the signal is real (+3.7pt 10-day, +0.5pt 14-day) on the 46%-coverage subset available now. The natural next measurement is the same clean test at close to 100% coverage — the effect size should either hold, confirming a genuine production feature, or shrink, which would itself be informative about whether the 4-country subset tested so far is unusually predictable.

  2. 2

    Try country-mean or region-mean imputation for uncovered WorldPop cells, as a bridge until the download finishes

    The indicator-flag fix only partially worked (helped at 14-day, not 10-day). A smarter fill value than zero — e.g. each country's own mean population density among its covered cells — is a cheap, untested alternative that doesn't require waiting on bandwidth-bound downloads.

  3. 3

    Deploy the 2-model stack (RF+XGB, LightGBM dropped) at 10-day, in production

    Confirmed neutral this round (78.6% vs. 78.8%) — a real simplification (fewer models to train, version, and maintain) with no measured cost. Low-risk, ready now.

  4. 4

    Stop investigating joint-filtered training; it's a closed question

    Confirmed negative at both horizons and both thresholds tested this round, by a wide and consistent margin. Future rounds should not re-test this without a specific new reason to expect a different result.

  5. 5

    Re-frame the severity task around its own realistic operating point instead of the occurrence task's 80% target

    Six rounds of severity results, across every architecture tried (single-stage, two-stage, matched-winner, averaged, LightGBM-specific), have topped out under 2% recall at anything near 80% precision. Reporting the full severity precision-recall curve and picking an operating point calibrated to what's actually achievable (perhaps 50-60% precision) — rather than continuing to inherit the occurrence task's 80% target by convention — would give a more honest picture of what severity forecasting can currently deliver.

  6. 6

    Test whether the ≥100 support floor's 10-day peak is stable out-of-sample

    Group B locates a clean peak on this round's single holdout window. A rolling re-estimate of the optimal floor across multiple holdout windows (rather than the one 60/40 split used throughout this project) would confirm whether ≥100 is a stable structural optimum or specific to this particular split date.
