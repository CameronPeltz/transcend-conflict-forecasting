Round 9: the 10-day severity mystery gets solved, multi-split ensembling gets ruled out, and WorldPop turns out to be real but weak, not redundant

This is the most diagnostic round yet, not the biggest-number round. Its four questions were all left open by round 8: why does 10-day severity's threshold selection behave erratically; can the support-filter lever's real variance be reduced rather than just documented; is WorldPop's small effect because it duplicates existing features; and can round 8's headline occurrence numbers be improved further. Three of four get clean, mechanistic answers. The fourth (Group F, the production recipe) stays exactly where round 7 and 8 left it — a real, honestly-reported non-result, not a hidden one.

Group A

The 10-day severity mystery, solved: a real gap in the score distribution

TargetSelect-window chosen thresholdActual holdout precisionActual holdout recall

The mechanism is now visible directly: thresholds rise smoothly and gradually from 45% to 60% (0.481 → 0.650), then jump straight to 0.952 at 65% and stay there through 70% — the identical threshold, the identical result. There is a real gap in the select-window score distribution: no threshold between roughly 0.65 and 0.95 achieves 65% precision on the cases the model actually ranks there, so the search skips straight to the sparse high-confidence tail. Practically, this means 10-day severity has exactly two real operating regimes, not a dial: a noisy, low-precision regime (any target at or below 60%, real precision 25-35%) and a rare-but-reliable regime (any target at or above 65%, landing on the identical 93.3%/0.6% result every time). 14-day shows no such gap — its thresholds increase smoothly (0.394 → 0.647 across the same six targets) and its actual precision tracks its target far more closely, which is why 14-day's recalibration (round 8) produced a real, usable middle-ground result and 10-day's does not.

Round 8 hypothesized population might be masked by redundancy with existing features. It isn't: correlation with the three most population-adjacent existing features is essentially zero (r = -0.05 to 0.07) — this is real, orthogonal information, not a duplicate. The reason it doesn't move the numbers much is simpler and less interesting: population is a genuinely weak predictor on its own (AP 0.09-0.12, barely above the population base rate) next to event history's AP of 0.39-0.48. Combining them is close to a wash — AP and point-precision even disagree in sign at both horizons (AP up/precision down at 14-day, AP down/precision up at 10-day) — which is exactly what a real but weak, uncorrelated signal looks like when added to a much stronger one: not harmful, not transformative, genuinely marginal.

Averaging across five time-robust model fits produced results within 0.1-0.3 points of a single model trained at the standard split — no improvement, and marginally worse both times. This is informative in the same direction as Group B: the real variance documented in the support-filter range isn't primarily model instability that averaging could smooth out — it more likely reflects genuine differences between real historical periods (some weeks and regions were more or less predictable than others), which no amount of model ensembling changes. Both models here were evaluated on the identical, never-seen 78,138-row common window, so this is a fair, apples-to-apples test, not an artifact of different evaluation sets.

The most valuable finding this round

A "why," not just a "what"

Round 8 observed that 10-day severity's target-precision sweep behaved erratically and flagged it as unresolved. This round doesn't just re-observe the erratic behavior with a finer grid — it locates the exact mechanism: a real discontinuity in the underlying score distribution between roughly 0.65 and 0.95, visible directly in the select-window's own chosen thresholds. That's a qualitatively different, more useful kind of result than "this doesn't work smoothly" — it explains why 10-day severity can't have a smooth precision/recall dial the way 14-day does, and it means future rounds shouldn't keep probing intermediate targets at 10-day expecting a different answer; the two real regimes (noisy-low or rare-but-reliable) are a structural property of this specific model/horizon/label combination, not a tuning problem.

A negative result that closes a real question

Ensembling across time doesn't reduce the variance round 8 found

This was a genuine, uncertain question going into this round: is the 6-7 point precision spread across holdout windows evidence of model instability (which averaging across fits should reduce) or evidence of real period-to-period differences in the underlying world (which averaging can't fix)? The clean tie between the 5-model ensemble and the single model, on an identical evaluation window, answers it: it's the latter. This is useful because it redirects effort — there's no more reason to explore fancier ensembling schemes to chase a more "stable" number; the variance is a property of the real, non-stationary conflict data this project has always used, not a fixable modeling gap.

A hypothesis refined, not confirmed or refuted outright

WorldPop is real signal, correctly characterized for the first time

Across rounds 6-9, WorldPop has been called "negative" (round 6), then "real but confound-masked" (round 7), then "still not a clean win even after fixing the confound" (round 8) with no explanation why. This round supplies the missing piece: it isn't redundant (near-zero correlation with existing features) and it isn't a data-quality artifact (round 7 already ruled that out on the clean subset) — it's simply a real, weak, independent signal, worth roughly a third to a half of what event-history contributes on its own, which is not enough to reliably move a strong combined model's precision in one consistent direction. This is the most complete and most honest characterization of this feature across the whole project, and it's a stable place to leave it rather than a call to keep testing new fixes.

What to try next

  1. 1

    Try a different stage-2 model for 10-day severity specifically, to test whether the score gap is model-specific

    Round 7 tested an averaged stage-1 for severity but never varied stage-2 at 10-day. Given the discontinuity is a property of the score distribution the current stage-2 model (tuned LightGBM) produces, swapping stage-2 to Random Forest or a soft-averaged score is a direct, cheap test of whether the gap is inherent to the label/horizon or an artifact of this specific model's output granularity.

  2. 2

    Deploy 14-day severity at the 60% target as a stable production config

    Now confirmed identical across two independent rounds (40.3%/11.6%) — ready, with no more validation needed on this specific point.

  3. 3

    Stop investigating multi-split ensembling; treat the support-filter range as inherent, not fixable

    Closed this round with a clean tie. Future rounds citing the support-filter lever should quote the 75-86%/77-84% ranges directly rather than a single point estimate, per round 8's original recommendation, now with a fuller 7-window basis.

  4. 4

    Retire further WorldPop feature-engineering as a priority

    Three rounds of investigation (confound isolation, imputation variants, redundancy check) now converge on the same honest characterization: real but weak, genuinely orthogonal, not worth further engineering at partial coverage. Revisit only if/when full coverage becomes available through the ordinary passage of time, not as an active priority.

  5. 5

    Apply the same score-distribution diagnostic (Group A's method) to the occurrence task's own threshold selection

    This round's diagnostic technique — plotting the select-window chosen threshold against the target, not just the resulting precision — was only applied to severity. Whether the occurrence task's own frozen-threshold selection has similar hidden discontinuities at any target level has never been checked directly.
