What the field already knows, what a rigorous evaluation should demand, and 24 real attempts to do better

Five tabs: a grounded review of where conflict forecasting research actually stands, a panel of field-specific evaluation standards translated to this problem, a standards-informed ranking of methods including forecasts several months out, a full log of 24 real backtested iterations on real data, and a closing summary with real case studies of what worked.

25-year arc

From coded events to graphs and language models

Conflict forecasting has gone through roughly four overlapping waves, each one adding a capability the last one lacked rather than fully replacing it — all four are still in active use today.

Wave 1 — expert-coded event data (1990s–2000s)

The Political Instability Task Force (PITF), a US government-funded research effort, built some of the first systematic event datasets by relying on human event coders reading news reports. This established the basic idea the whole field still runs on: turn a news report into a structured record — who did what to whom, when, where — rather than trying to forecast from raw text directly.

Wave 2 — automated, machine-coded events at scale (2010s)

ICEWS and GDELT both automated that coding process using natural-language processing against the CAMEO taxonomy (the same 20-category event-type scheme used throughout this project), trading per-event precision for enormous scale and near-real-time updates. This is also when ACLED and UCDP's more narrowly-scoped, human-curated, fatality-focused datasets became the field's standard ground truth for evaluating everything else against.

Wave 3 — machine learning on top of coded events (mid-2010s–present)

Researchers found that tree-based ensemble methods — random forests, gradient boosting — reliably outperformed classical logistic regression on the rare-event, class-imbalanced nature of conflict onset prediction. ViEWS (the Violence & Impacts Early-Warning System, a live public system run jointly by Uppsala University and the Peace Research Institute Oslo since 2017) built on this directly: it now issues monthly, publicly-published forecasts of conflict fatalities 1–36 months ahead, for every country and for 55×55 km grid cells across Africa and the Middle East, using an ensemble of multiple tree-based models deliberately built to disagree with each other ("variance-promoting data configurations," in the field's own language) rather than one single best model.

Wave 4 — graph-native and language-model approaches (2020s, accelerating in 2025–2026)

Published work on spatio-temporal graph convolutional networks applied directly to event data — the same shape of technique as the temporal-graph-neural-network approach discussed and deliberately set aside in this project's earlier documents — has shown real promise for capturing relational structure conventional feature-flattening misses. More recently, several 2025–2026 papers mark a genuine, active shift toward retrieval-augmented and language-model-based forecasting specifically: research directly asking "do large language models know conflict?" by comparing what's baked into a model's pretrained weights against retrieval-augmented approaches; a framework called LLM4Geopolitics that explicitly couples a domain-adapted language model with retrieval-augmented generation grounded in a structured knowledge graph — architecturally close to what this project's own documents have been recommending independently; and comparative studies of text-based methods (topic modeling on newspaper articles, actor-embedding transformer networks) for exactly the kind of crisis early-warning use case this whole project targets.

Best practices

What the strongest systems consistently do

  • Forecast out-of-time, in public, before the answer is known. ViEWS publishes its forecasts on a fixed schedule and lets outcomes catch up to them later — the strongest possible discipline against hindsight bias, and one that this project's own rolling-origin backtests are a smaller-scale version of.
  • Build ensembles for genuine diversity, not just averaging. ViEWS's own stated approach — multiple tree-based algorithms plus data configurations deliberately chosen to make different mistakes — is a specific, real, citable precedent that this project directly tests in its own iteration log (tab 04).
  • Report uncertainty, not just a point estimate. The most recent public ViEWS prediction challenge is explicitly framed around forecasting fatalities "with uncertainty" — a probabilistic range, not a single number — matching how meteorology and epidemiology both report forecasts (see tab 02).
  • Predict at more than one spatial resolution. ViEWS's parallel country-level and 55×55 km grid-cell forecasts are a real, working example of resolution flexibility this project's own country-week panel doesn't yet attempt — a genuine opportunity, not just a nice-to-have (see below).
Gaps

Where the field has real, documented limits

The early-warning-to-early-action gap. This is a named, widely-discussed problem in the field, not a minor caveat: AI-powered forecasting can genuinely detect rising risk, but without the political will and institutional mechanisms to act on a warning, the forecast itself changes nothing. Worth stating plainly in any proposal built on top of this research — a better model does not by itself solve the harder half of the problem.
  • Nearly every major dataset is downstream of wire-service and mainstream-media reporting. ICEWS, GDELT, and PITF all ultimately depend on what English-language and major regional news outlets chose to cover. That's precisely the blind spot in exactly the low-media-access, data-sparse regions this project's underlying program is meant to address — the literature confirms the gap is real, not just an assumption behind the program's premise.
  • Automated coding quality varies a lot, and it's a known, acknowledged tradeoff. ICEWS is documented as using deliberately loose inclusion criteria to maximize coverage; GDELT's own documentation and this project's own reference document both note it's noisier and more duplicate-prone than hand-coded alternatives like ACLED. Free and fast is not the same as free and clean.
  • Language-model calibration for this specific task is an open research question, not a settled one. The "do LLMs know conflict" line of research exists specifically because it isn't yet clear how much of an LLM's apparent forecasting skill is real signal versus memorized patterns from training data that happen to correlate with outcomes — worth real caution before treating an LLM-based forecast as inherently trustworthy just because the RFP asks for one.
  • Rare-event, small-sample statistics are a structural problem across the whole field, not unique to any one project. Conflict escalation is rare by definition; every serious system in this space — including ViEWS, running on decades of data across the whole world — deals with severe class imbalance. This project's own small prototype scale (tab 04) inherits that same structural challenge, just more acutely.
Opportunities

Where new methods could plausibly move the field forward

  • Retrieval-augmented, graph-grounded language models — no longer speculative; LLM4Geopolitics is a real, published 2026 framework doing close to exactly this, which is real external validation that the architecture recommended throughout this project's earlier documents is a live research direction, not a niche bet.
  • Genuinely local, non-English, non-wire-service signal capture — radio, community reporting, local-language sources — remains comparatively unexplored in the published literature reviewed here, which is itself informative: it suggests the specific novel contribution this project's underlying program is funding is a real, underserved gap, not a solved problem being reinvented.
  • Multi-resolution spatial modeling, following ViEWS's grid-cell precedent, is a natural extension once a project like this one outgrows country-level granularity.
  • Systematic ensemble-diversity testing, rather than picking one model family and tuning it, is a real, field-validated practice this project can test directly and cheaply — see tab 04, iterations 8–10.

Format

A standards panel, convened on paper

Five illustrative roles, not real named individuals — each one a composite voice for real, documented, verifiable standards from a field that already does probabilistic forecasting of rare, high-stakes events at scale. The point isn't the personas; it's importing what each field has already learned the hard way about evaluating exactly this kind of prediction.

The panel: a political-violence forecasting researcher (ViEWS-style academic conflict modeling) · an operational meteorologist specializing in forecast verification · a coordinator from an infectious-disease forecasting hub · a model-risk-management lead from financial-services regulation · an all-source intelligence analyst versed in analytic tradecraft standards.
Session 1

What should the primary metric even be?

Forecasting researcherPolitical violence, ML ensembles
Precision and recall get quoted the most in conflict-ML papers, but they're threshold-dependent and unstable on rare events with small samples. I'd rather see the Brier score and its decomposition front and center — it rewards genuinely calibrated probabilities, not just a lucky ranking.
MeteorologistForecast verification
Agreed on Brier, with a caveat. We never report it alone — always alongside a reliability diagram, plotting predicted probability against observed frequency in bins. A model can have a fine Brier score overall while being badly overconfident in exactly the high-probability bin that matters most operationally. And always report a skill score relative to a reference forecast — raw Brier means nothing without knowing what "no skill" looks like on this specific problem.
Forecast hub coordinatorInfectious disease
We standardized on the weighted interval score for exactly this reason — it's a proper scoring rule that rewards sharp, well-calibrated interval forecasts, not just point probabilities, and it decomposes into over- and under-prediction penalties you can diagnose separately. I'd push this group toward reporting a real uncertainty range on any fatality or severity estimate, not just an escalation yes/no.
Model-risk leadFinancial services
From where I sit, the single metric matters less than the backtesting protocol around it. Regulatory backtesting for risk models counts exceptions — actual outcomes falling outside the model's predicted range — over a fixed rolling window, and classifies the model into a green/yellow/red zone based on how many exceptions occurred. I'd want this group's models formally re-certified or flagged the same way, on a fixed cadence, not just backtested once and shipped.
Intelligence analystAll-source analysis, tradecraft standards
All of that is necessary but not sufficient for how this actually gets used. Analytic tradecraft standards require every judgment to state its confidence level in plain, standardized language — not a bare number a policymaker has to interpret. A well-calibrated 0.62 means nothing to a decision-maker without a shared vocabulary for what that number is supposed to mean in practice.
Session 2

What does a fair backtest actually require?

Forecasting researcher
Non-negotiable: strict out-of-time validation. Random k-fold cross-validation is a real and common mistake in this literature specifically because conflict events cluster in time and space — a random split leaks neighboring information across the train/test boundary and inflates every metric.
Meteorologist
We'd call that "look-ahead bias" and it's the first thing any verification review checks for. The other thing we insist on: stratify performance by lead time. A forecast that's excellent at 24 hours and worthless at 5 days shouldn't get to report one blended number.
Model-risk lead
And a model has to clear a defined floor before it's allowed to claim skill at all — in our world that's usually "beats the previous approved model" or "beats a naive constant forecast." This group's naive-persistence and historical-base-rate models are exactly that discipline, and I'd insist every single iteration report the delta against both, not just its own raw score.
Forecast hub coordinator
One more: report coverage — what fraction of the time did the real outcome fall inside your stated uncertainty range at the level you claimed. A model that's "80% confident" should be right about 80% of the time it says that, not 50% and not 95%. That's a directly checkable, falsifiable claim, and it's checked automatically every week in our world.
Session 3

What counts as an acceptable explanation of a result?

Intelligence analyst
In our world, a judgment without sourcing and an explicit confidence level isn't usable, no matter how good the underlying model is. I'd hold any LLM-based forecaster in this project to the same bar: a reasoning trace isn't optional flavor text, it's the actual deliverable a human has to be able to challenge.
Model-risk lead
Financial regulation calls this "model risk management documentation" — every model in production needs a written record of its assumptions, known limitations, and validation history that a second, independent party can review without talking to whoever built it. Worth this group adopting that discipline now, while the system is small, rather than trying to reconstruct it later.
Forecasting researcher
And feature importance or SHAP-style attribution, exactly like this project has already been using, is the tree-based-model equivalent of that same idea — it's what lets a skeptical reviewer check whether the model is keying on something sensible or something spurious.
Consensus

The checklist the panel converges on

Used directly in tab 03 to score this project's own methods, not just discussed in the abstract.

  • ✓Calibration first. Report Brier score (or equivalent) as the headline metric, not precision/recall alone.
  • ✓Always show the floor. Every result reported alongside naive persistence and historical base rate from the identical fold.
  • ✓Strict out-of-time validation. No random splits, ever, on temporally/spatially clustered event data.
  • ✓Stratify by lead time. Report 1-week, monthly, and multi-month performance separately, never blended.
  • ✓Report decision-relevant metrics too. Precision/recall/specificity at a real operating threshold, because calibration alone can hide a model that never actually flags anything (see iteration 23, tab 04).
  • ✓State confidence in plain, standardized language alongside any numeric probability, not instead of it.
  • ✓Document assumptions and known limitations in writing, reviewable independent of whoever built the model.
  • ✓Re-certify on a fixed cadence rather than treating one backtest as a permanent result.

Months, not just weeks

How far out can this actually forecast?

The panel's lead-time-stratification rule applied directly: the same core feature set and model, tested at 1 week, roughly 1 month, and roughly 2 months out, on the same real data.

An honest caveat the panel's own standards demand stating: average precision rising at longer horizons looks counterintuitive, and it's worth resisting the urge to declare "this model forecasts months out better than weeks out." The sample shrinks at each longer horizon (fewer usable folds, since each fold needs that many future weeks still available in the data) — 120 predictions at 1 week versus 78 at 8 weeks. Session 2's out-of-time-validation standard says a metric computed on a meaningfully different, smaller sample isn't directly comparable to one computed on a larger one, even when both used the same rolling-origin discipline. The honest reading: there's real, usable signal out to roughly 2 months in this data — not yet an honest claim about which horizon is "most accurate."
What comes next

How to keep testing new grouping and weighting ideas systematically

The panel's ensemble-diversity and floor-comparison standards point toward a concrete, repeatable method for generating new ideas worth testing, rather than guessing one at a time. Four real categories of variation, each with several concrete instances actually tested in tab 04:

  1. Vary the event-grouping taxonomy — the standard 4-bucket cooperation/conflict split (quad-class) versus a severity-weighted regrouping straight from the real 20-category CAMEO taxonomy (which specific categories count as "severe"), versus single-signal groupings (tone alone, hostility score alone, raw volume alone) to see how much each family of signal carries on its own.
  2. Vary how much history and how coarse a time window each prediction sees — weekly versus two-week aggregation, two versus three weeks of lagged history feeding each forecast.
  3. Vary how sensitive the definition of "escalation" itself is — a looser statistical trigger catches more real cases at the cost of more borderline ones; a stricter one does the reverse. This is a real, independent axis of variation from anything about the model itself.
  4. Vary the model family and how predictions get combined — boosted trees at different depths and regularization strengths, bagged trees, linear models, and genuine multi-model ensembles, plus a calibration pass on top of the best single model, directly testing session 1's calibration-first standard.

Lessons learned

What 24 real iterations actually taught

  1. 1

    Identity beats volume of features

    Simply telling the model which country it's scoring (#2) beat throwing every possible feature at it (#16). Knowing who mattered more than knowing more.

  2. 2

    More history helped; more model complexity mostly didn't

    A third week of lagged history (#18) was the second-best real result in the batch. Deeper trees (#4), more features (#16), and bigger ensembles (#10) mostly didn't earn their added complexity.

  3. 3

    Calibration and decision-usefulness are genuinely different goals

    The best-calibrated real model (#23) flagged nothing at all. Any future deployment needs both numbers reported side by side, permanently — never one alone.

  4. 4

    The label definition is its own lever, separate from the model

    Loosening or tightening what counts as "escalation" (#19, #20) moved the numbers as much as any modeling choice did. Worth as much deliberate attention as model selection.

  5. 5

    Real signal plausibly extends to two months out — with an honest asterisk

    Average precision held up, and by some readings improved, out to an 8-week horizon (#22) — genuinely encouraging for the RFP's multi-week ambitions, but the shrinking sample at longer horizons means this needs re-testing on more data before being treated as settled.

Where this points next

The concrete next iteration

  • Combine #18 (3-week lag) + #2 (country identity) + #9 (GBM/Random Forest ensemble) in one configuration — the three strongest, sample-comparable, independently-real results from this round, never yet tested together.
  • Re-run the calibration test (#23) on that combined configuration specifically — a model worth deploying needs to pass both the calibration bar and the decision-usefulness bar at the same time, and this round only ever tested calibration on the plain baseline.
  • Re-test the longer-horizon findings (#21, #22) once more history is available, per tab 03's sample-size caution, before treating "this model sees two months out" as a settled result rather than a promising early one.
  • Wire in a live language-model call for the retrieval-based forecaster and re-run this same 24-configuration structure against it — every iteration here used gradient-boosted trees or simpler; the RFP's actual required mechanism has not yet been run through this same rigorous a testing pass.
The question this tab answers

"Gather way more data, model causality in general, then find conflict's leading indicators — that should produce a better model, right?"

The honest answer turned out to be more interesting than a plain yes or no: real, freely available structural data was gathered and it did produce the best real result in the whole project — but not through general-purpose causal modeling, and the formal causality tests themselves came back mostly negative. Both halves of that are reported below, not just the flattering half.

What was deliberately not done, and why: a general-purpose "predict any event, then see what transfers to conflict" model, and a claim of confirmed causation. Observational news/event data can't support real causal identification without a research design this project doesn't have (see the prior conversation turn), and pretraining on all events is an unbounded, largely orthogonal problem. What follows instead is targeted: real new datasets, tested directly against the existing conflict label, using both a formal leading-indicator test (Granger-style) and a direct predictive backtest — the same rolling-origin discipline used everywhere else in this project.

Formal leading-indicator test

Granger-style causality testing — the honest, mostly-null results

For each new signal: does its own recent history improve prediction of next-week conflict intensity, beyond what conflict's own history already predicts? Two versions run — a classical per-country test (real but statistically weak with ~26 weeks per country) and a pooled fixed-effects version borrowing power across all six countries (more power, weaker assumption).

The honest statistical caveat this result demands: 4 signals × 6 countries = 24 separate per-country tests were run. At the conventional p<0.05 threshold, roughly 1 false positive is expected by chance alone even if nothing here were real — so the two apparent hits (Venezuela rainfall, Afghanistan GKG themes) are exactly what pure chance would predict, not confirmed discoveries. Neither survives in the pooled, more-powered version of the same test. The rigorous, disciplined answer is: this round's formal causality-style testing did not find a generalizable leading indicator — a real, disclosed null result, not a failure to report.
Direct predictive test

Does the new data help the actual model, independent of the Granger result?

Granger significance and predictive usefulness in a nonlinear model are different questions — a signal can help gradient-boosted trees find a useful nonlinear split without producing a significant linear Granger F-test, and vice versa. Synthesis

So — was the premise right?

  • Gathering more data: yes, clearly worth it. Four new real, freely-available sources were integrated in one round, and one of them (election proximity) produced the single best real result in the project so far.
  • Modeling causality in general, for any event, first: still the wrong lever. Nothing in this round needed a general-purpose causal or all-event model — the win came from directly testing specific, well-motivated candidate signals against the specific outcome that matters, exactly as argued before building any of this.
  • The causality-style testing itself mostly said "no": a disciplined Granger-style test, run honestly with a multiple-comparisons caveat, did not find a generalizable leading indicator among the four new sources. That's a real, valuable finding on its own — it means the election-proximity win is better understood as a strong direct predictor discovered through backtesting, not as a confirmed causal driver, and shouldn't be oversold as one.
  • The "more features together" trap keeps reappearing. This is now the second time (after iteration #16 in the original study) that combining every available feature family performed worse than a couple of well-chosen ones — a real, repeated, small-sample lesson worth trusting going forward.

Next real step: re-run the calibration test (as in the original study's iteration #23) on the new best configuration specifically, since a model isn't deployment-ready until it passes both the ranking bar and the calibration bar at once — and extend the election calendar with verified dates further out as they're announced, since it's currently the strongest single lever found.
