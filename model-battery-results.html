Definitions

What each metric actually answers

Why accuracy alone is misleading here

A model that predicts "no escalation" for every single country-week would be right about 84.2% of the time, since real escalation is rare (~14–16% base rate). Several models below score below that trivial floor while still being far more useful — because they're actually trying to catch the rare positive cases, and every one they correctly flag costs some accuracy in exchange.

Precision

Of the country-weeks flagged as escalation-risk, what share actually escalated. High precision means an analyst acting on the alert isn't wasting attention.

Recall

Of the country-weeks that actually escalated, what share got flagged in advance. High recall means fewer real escalations slip through unflagged.

Specificity

Of the country-weeks that stayed calm, what share were correctly left unflagged. Directly answers "how many of your negatives were true negatives" — the complement of the false-alarm rate.

Brier score

How close the predicted probabilities land to the real outcomes, on a 0–1 scale where lower is better. The only metric here that rewards good calibration rather than just a good yes/no call.

Ranking

So which one actually wins?

Honestly: it depends what the flagged list is for. No single model wins every column, and that disagreement is itself the finding worth reporting rather than picking a favorite metric after the fact.

  1. 1

    ICL over the temporal graph — best all-around at the 1-week horizon

    Best Brier, best accuracy, best precision, and best specificity among models that actually flag anything. The one model here that's both reasonably calibrated and decision-useful at 1 week — though see tab 03 for why this is currently a hand-tuned formula standing in for a real LLM call, not a demonstrated NLP-reasoning advantage yet.

  2. 2

    Logistic regression — the one to reach for if recall matters most

    Best average precision at 2 weeks, and by far the best recall at both horizons (0.737 and 0.611) — it catches the most real escalations. The cost is severe: specificity as low as 0.416, meaning it's wrong more often than right when it flags something. Useful for a "cast a wide net, human reviews everything" workflow; not for an automated alert.

  3. 3

    GBM on graph features — solid, but didn't lead this round

    Middling on every metric at both horizons, and notably didn't beat the ICL forecaster's calibration this time despite being the retrained, "advantaged" baseline. Tab 04 has a specific, fixable reason why.

  4. 4

    Naive persistence — beaten by nearly everything

    "Conflict continues where it's already happening" is a weak assumption at weekly granularity in this sample — mediocre across every column, exactly the kind of floor the methodology expects a real model to clear.

  5. 5

    Historical base rate — best on paper, least useful in practice

    Wins Brier and accuracy at both horizons by construction, and catches zero real escalations at the 0.5 threshold. Kept in the battery specifically because it exposes this — the reference document's insistence on running it every fold, not just once, is doing real work here.

The honest headline: at 1-week, the corrected RFP-required approach (ICL over the graph) is the actual best model in this battery on real, if still modest-scale, data — not just the one the solicitation happens to require. That wasn't guaranteed going in, and it's a stronger result than the first, smaller backtest showed.

Honest limitation

None of these five models actually traverse the graph yet

Worth stating plainly rather than glossing over: every model in this battery, including the ICL forecaster, currently operates on a flattened summary of the graph — event counts, quad-class shares, an actor-count proxy — not the graph's actual edge structure. The reference document's schema defines ALLIED_WITH / HOSTILE_TO edges with time-scoped validity windows and PRECEDED_BY / ESCALATED_FROM event-chain edges specifically so a model could reason about relationships, not just counts. Nothing built so far reads those edges.

What this means concretely: the 232,998-node, 622,505-edge graph built in this round is real and does get constructed (build_features.py's build_graph() function), but the feature-engineering step downstream of it — build_panel() — only asks "how many distinct actors touched an event this week," never "which two actors newly started appearing in the same events together" or "did an alliance edge flip hostile." That second kind of question is what would make this a genuinely graph-native approach rather than a graph-flattened one. It's the single highest-value piece of unfinished work from the reference document's own schema — see tab 04.
Reference doc, tab 01

Signal-based vs. LLM-based, compared against real results

The reference document frames these as complementary families — "the strongest systems run both." This round's results let that claim actually be checked against real numbers instead of just argued in the abstract.

A. Signal-based (GBM on graph features)

What it's good at, confirmed: full auditability. Every prediction traces back to a ranked, numeric feature-importance table (tab 02) — a reviewer can see exactly why a score moved. That's a real, demonstrated strength, not a theoretical one.

What it struggled with this round: didn't lead on any metric at either horizon, and its precision at 1 week (0.273) trailed the ICL forecaster's (0.375) despite being the model allowed to retrain every fold. Tab 04 has a specific, addressable reason.

B. LLM-based inference (ICL forecaster, currently in fallback mode)

What it's good at, confirmed: best overall balance at 1 week — calibration, precision, and specificity all led the battery. The historical-analog retrieval step (real, not simulated — genuine cosine similarity over standardized features against the training-window index) appears to be doing real work.

The honest caveat, repeated because it matters: no ANTHROPIC_API_KEY was available in this environment, so every one of these predictions ran through the documented fallback formula in models.py, not a real language model. The result above is therefore a real demonstration that retrieval-augmented scoring works reasonably well on this data — it is not yet evidence that LLM-style reasoning specifically adds value over the fallback's fixed arithmetic. That comparison needs a live key.

What changes with a live model

What a real LLM call would actually add over the fallback

  • Qualitative context the numeric features can't see. A real model reading the serialized context (tab 03 of the prior document) could weigh a specific actor name, a specific event type, or a phrasing pattern the quad-class/Goldstein numbers compress away — the reference document's own stated case for LLM inference.
  • A reasoning trace worth auditing, not just logging. Right now the "reasoning" field records which formula ran. A live model's reasoning paragraph would be the actual artifact a human analyst reviews before acting — the RFP's implicit deliverable, not a debugging log.
  • A real test of calibration under language-model uncertainty. LLMs are known to be poorly calibrated probability estimators without extra work (temperature tuning, ensembling, or explicit calibration against held-out data) — worth testing directly rather than assuming, especially since the fallback's calibration already set a real bar (Brier 0.171 / 0.162) to beat.
Prioritized

What would move accuracy the most from here

  • 1

    Give the GBM a country identity

    The current feature set (tab 02) is entirely trailing statistics — the model never actually sees which country it's scoring. That's likely the specific, fixable reason it underperformed the ICL forecaster's country-implicit historical-analog retrieval this round. Adding country as a categorical feature (as already done in the pooled property-prediction work referenced by this same methodology) is a same-day change.

  • 2

    Make the graph features actually graph-native

    Tab 02's core finding: nothing here reads an edge yet. Concrete next features: a week-over-week count of new actor pairs co-occurring in events (proxying the reference document's "network centrality shifts"), and a real implementation of the schema's ALLIED_WITH / HOSTILE_TO time-scoped edges so a shift in that relationship is directly queryable rather than inferred from raw actor counts.

  • 3

    Wire in the live LLM call

    Repeated from the prior document because this round's results make it more urgent, not less — the ICL forecaster is currently winning on a fallback formula, and the actual question the RFP cares about (does in-context learning over the graph work) is still untested against a real model.

  • 4

    Move off the 0.5 threshold

    Every model's precision/recall/specificity numbers above are read at a single, arbitrary cutoff that clearly isn't right for a ~15% base rate — it's why logistic regression looks simultaneously "best recall" and "worst everything else." A precision-recall curve swept across thresholds, with the operating point chosen deliberately for the actual downstream use (automated alert vs. analyst worklist), would make every comparison in tab 01 sharper.

  • 5

    Register for ACLED, replace the proxy label

    Still the highest-leverage ground-truth fix available — a fatality-coded, hand-curated onset date directly replaces the Goldstein/quad-class trailing-baseline proxy this whole battery has been trained against, and matches the RFP's own stated evaluation standard exactly.

  • 6

    Keep expanding real history

    55 → 160 country-weeks already changed the ranking meaningfully between the two backtest rounds. GDELT 2.0's full 2015–present history is still sitting there unused; a multi-year pull is the most direct way to turn "23 positive labels" into a sample size that can support more than 6 features without real overfitting risk.

Four real weeks where things turned bad — and how five different forecasting approaches each read the warning signs

What's being measured, and who's doing the guessing

What counts as "escalation" here

A country having a noticeably worse week than its own recent normal — a real, sharp jump in hostile or violent activity compared to how that country usually looks. Each case below is a real week where that jump happened after the prediction point — the outcome was checked only after every model had already made its guess, so nothing below is hindsight.

The five ways of guessing, translated

All five were given the exact same information and asked the same question — "how likely is this country to have a bad week next week?" — they just go about answering it very differently:

  • The pattern-trained model (labeled "GBM" in the charts below, short for "gradient-boosted trees"): studied hundreds of past country-weeks and learned, by trial and error, which combinations of warning signs tended to come before trouble. Like a weather forecaster who's built up instinct from years of watching pressure systems.
  • The similar-past-cases lookup ("ICL", short for "in-context learning"): does no learning or training at all. Instead, for each new week, it searches back through every country's history for the handful of past weeks that looked most similar, checks how many of those actually led to trouble, and uses that as its answer. Like asking "what happened the last five times we saw something like this, anywhere?" This is the approach the government program funding this research specifically asks for.
  • The simple trend formula ("LogReg", short for "logistic regression"): the plainest possible math — mostly just "activity has been going up or down by this much lately" — with no ability to weigh one signal more heavily than another the way the pattern-trained model can.
  • "Assume nothing changes" (labeled "Persistence"): the laziest honest guess — if a country was already having a bad week, assume next week looks the same. Not a real model, just a floor every real approach needs to beat.
  • "Use the country's own long-run average" (labeled "Base rate"): guesses based purely on how often that specific country has historically had bad weeks — ignoring whatever is happening right now entirely. The other floor every real approach needs to beat.

The warning signs each approach was looking at

  • Event count — how many reported incidents of any kind (friendly, neutral, or hostile) happened in that country that week.
  • Share of events that were real conflict — of those incidents, what fraction were actual physical/violent actions rather than statements, meetings, or diplomacy. A country can have the same number of "events" one week to the next while the mix quietly shifts from talk to action.
  • Average hostility score (called "Goldstein score" in the technical tabs) — researchers rate every reported incident on a fixed scale, from very cooperative at the positive end to very violent at the negative end. Averaging that score across a whole week gives a single read on how peaceful or hostile the week was overall.
  • News tone — separate from what actually happened, how negative or alarmed the news coverage of it sounded.
  • Number of distinct people/groups involved — a rough read on how many different players are mixed up in a situation, versus it being contained to just one or two.
  • "Change from the week before" — for every signal above, what matters isn't just the number itself but whether it moved sharply up or down compared to the prior week.

The clearest real example of why "look up similar past cases" earns its place: the "country's own average" guess literally could not have caught this — Sudan's own history contained zero bad weeks to learn from, so it confidently predicted calm, and was wrong. The lookup model isn't limited to one country's own history, though — it searches every country's past for weeks that looked similar, regardless of which country they happened in, found that two out of five of those cross-country matches went on to get worse, and combined that with the same hostility-score warning signs. That's the one structural advantage this approach has over anything trained only on a single country's own track record: it can recognize a pattern in a country having its first bad week ever, because it's seen the pattern play out somewhere else before.

An honest, clean miss — worth admitting plainly rather than explaining away: the lookup model searched for similar past weeks and found that literally none of the closest matches had led to trouble, which pushed its guess down rather than up. Every approach missed this one for the same underlying reason, not five different reasons: whatever actually drove this particular escalation left no visible fingerprint in any of the warning signs being tracked — not the incident count, not the hostility score, not the news tone. That's not a case of the models guessing badly; it's a case of the information they had access to simply not containing the answer. It's exactly the kind of gap that richer, more local sources of information — the actual radio broadcasts and community reporting this whole research program exists to start capturing — are meant to close.
Pattern across all four

What these four cases add up to

  • The pattern-trained model does best when trouble shows up as a shift in the mix of what's happening (Myanmar) — fewer incidents, but a rising share of them turning violent. It's seen that specific shape before and knows to weight it heavily, even when a simpler approach would read the falling raw numbers as good news.
  • The look-up-similar-cases approach does best when a country is heading into unfamiliar territory for the first time (Sudan) — because it isn't limited to that one country's own history the way a country-specific average is. This is the single clearest reason the approach earns the emphasis it gets in this research.
  • Every approach struggles the same way when activity is still high, just less high than an extreme spike (Colombia) — a real, fixable weak spot: these methods currently pay more attention to which direction things are trending than to how much is still actually happening.
  • Every approach fails the same way when the real warning sign was never captured in the first place (Venezuela) — not something better modeling can fix on this data. It's a case for gathering richer information, not smarter math.
