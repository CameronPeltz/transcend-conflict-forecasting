Plain-English glossary

What each metric on this page actually means

Every metric below is answering a slightly different question about the same underlying situation: each week, for each country, the model outputs a number between 0 and 1 (its estimated chance of an escalation), and either an escalation really happened that week or it didn't. "Flagged" below means the model's probability crossed the 0.5 cutoff and it effectively said "watch this one."

The four things that can happen, every time the model makes a call

Every metric on this page is built from just four counts, tallied up across every real prediction: flagged it, and it escalated (a correct catch), flagged it, but it stayed quiet (a false alarm), didn't flag it, and it stayed quiet (a correct pass), and didn't flag it, but it escalated (a missed crisis — the worst kind of mistake for an early-warning system). Everything below is just different ways of adding up and comparing those four counts.

Accuracy higher is better

Out of every prediction the model made, what fraction were correct — including both "correctly flagged a real escalation" and "correctly left a quiet week alone"?

The trap: if only 15% of weeks ever escalate, a model that flags nothing, ever is still "85% accurate" while catching zero real crises. That's exactly why this page never reports accuracy by itself — always check it next to recall.

Precision higher is better

Of the weeks the model actually flagged as "watch this one," what fraction turned out to be real? Answers: "when it cries wolf, how often is it right?"

Precision 0.69 means about 7 out of every 10 alerts the model raised were genuine — the other 3 were false alarms.

Recall higher is better

Of the weeks that actually escalated, what fraction did the model catch? Answers: "how many real crises got missed?" (Also called sensitivity elsewhere.)

Recall 0.87 means the model caught 87 out of every 100 real escalations — and missed the other 13.

Specificity higher is better

The mirror image of recall, but for quiet weeks: of the weeks that actually stayed quiet, what fraction did the model correctly leave alone? Answers: "how good is it at not crying wolf?"

Specificity 0.85 means that out of 100 genuinely quiet weeks, the model correctly stayed silent on 85 of them and raised a false alarm on the other 15.

F1 score higher is better, 0–1

A single number that blends precision and recall together, so a model can't score well by being great at one while ignoring the other completely.

A model with high precision but near-zero recall (rare, cautious, but misses almost everything) gets a low F1 — the blend punishes lopsided performance.

Brier score lower is better, 0 = perfect

How far off the model's predicted probabilities were from reality, on average — not just whether it flagged something, but how confident it was and whether that confidence was justified.

Predicting a 90% chance of escalation and being right costs very little; predicting 90% and being wrong costs a lot. A model that always predicts "10% chance" and is usually right can beat a flashier model that's frequently overconfident.

Average Precision (AP) higher is better, 0–1

Also called PR-AUC. Measures how well the model ranks real escalations above false alarms, across every possible alert threshold at once, with extra weight on getting the very top of the ranking right. This is the main "how good is this model, overall" number used throughout this document.

A useful sanity check for AP: with escalations happening in roughly 15–30% of weeks depending on the track, a model that's just guessing randomly would score an AP near that same 0.15–0.30 baseline — so an AP of 0.87 (this round's best result) is meaningfully better than chance, not just a big-looking number.

ROC-AUC higher is better, 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect

If you grabbed one week that actually escalated and one that didn't, completely at random, ROC-AUC is the probability the model gave the escalating week the higher risk score.

ROC-AUC 0.93 means: pick any real escalation and any real quiet week at random, and 93% of the time the model correctly rated the escalation as riskier.

Log-loss lower is better, 0 = perfect

Similar to Brier score — how far off the predicted probabilities were — but it punishes confident, wrong answers much more harshly.

Predicting "1% chance of escalation" right before one happens is penalized far more severely under log-loss than under Brier score — it specifically discourages a model from ever being both confident and wrong.

MCC — Matthews Correlation Coefficient higher is better, -1 to +1

A single balanced score that accounts for all four outcomes (correct catches, false alarms, correct passes, missed crises) at once. 0 means no better than random guessing; 1 means perfect; negative means worse than random.

Unlike accuracy, MCC doesn't get fooled by a rare-event problem like this one — a "flag nothing, ever" model that looks great on accuracy scores 0 on MCC, correctly showing it has no real skill.

n / pos not a metric — the sample size behind one

n is the total number of real country-week predictions pooled together to compute that row's scores (across every rolling-origin fold, for one model configuration). pos is short for "positive" — standard statistics/ML shorthand for the "positive class," i.e. the outcome being detected — and here means how many of those n predictions were actual real escalations (label = 1); the rest were quiet weeks (the "negative" class, label = 0).

"1245 / 341" means the score was computed from 1,245 real predictions, 341 of which were genuine escalations. A row reading "18 / 2" is built from only 2 real escalation examples — technically a real score, but small enough that one lucky or unlucky guess can swing it a lot. That's exactly why tab 01's headline table only includes rows with at least 15 real positives, and why every "best result" claim in this document names its n/pos alongside the score.

Finding 2

The hypergraph model's value depends on how much signal the label already has

The hypergraph neural network is the best model family on Tracks A and B (mean AP 0.302 and 0.182, both #1) and the worst on Track C (mean AP 0.650, #6 of 6). That's not a contradiction — it's a real, interpretable pattern: Tracks A and B use a Goldstein/quad-class proxy label with a weak, diffuse tabular signal, exactly the condition where extra structure (who's connected to whom, this week, this region, this actor) has real information to add on top of a handful of lagged scalars. Track C's fatality-coded label is already strongly autocorrelated with simple lagged deaths and event counts — real violence has real momentum — so a GBM or ensemble already captures most of the exploitable signal, and the hypergraph's extra structure adds noise (more parameters, same effective sample) rather than signal.

Practical reading: reach for the hypergraph model specifically when working from a noisy proxy label with limited direct predictive signal — not as a universal upgrade over tabular models.
Finding 3

Label spreading is a real early-warning risk: it ranks well and alerts on nothing

Across all 68 real label-spreading runs this round, 39.7% produced exactly 0.000 recall at the standard 0.5 threshold — the model consistently ranks true escalation weeks above quiet ones (mean AP 0.30–0.71 depending on track, often near the top of a track's leaderboard) while almost never actually crossing the threshold to flag one. This is the calibration-vs-decision-usefulness tension this project's expert-panel review flagged in an earlier round, now reproduced concretely and repeatedly in a brand-new model family rather than as a one-off observation.

A model that "wins" on AP but alerts on nothing is actively dangerous to report as a leaderboard winner without this caveat — exactly the trap tab 01's headline table was filtered (n_pos≥15) to avoid, and exactly why every ranking in this document reports Brier, precision, AND recall side by side rather than a single number.

Real, verified

What the best models actually said before the outcome was known

Every case below used the real local Ollama (llama3.1) call path — genuine model-generated reasoning, not the documented heuristic fallback — checked afterward against the real, actual label for that country-week. The prediction was made using only information available strictly before the forecast window, per this project's bitemporal discipline throughout.

Colombia, week of 2026-04-27 — 1-week horizon

p = 0.55

Actual outcome: escalation occurred. Track A, native GDELT features (no reindexing involved — fully semantically accurate).

"The current week for country CO shows a significant increase in the number of events and material-conflict share compared to the previous week, suggesting an escalation in tensions. The decrease in the Mean Goldstein score also indicates a more conflictual situation. However, considering the historical analogs, while 40% of similar weeks led to escalation, only two out of five most similar weeks to the current state resulted in actual conflicts, which might indicate that the probability is slightly lower than expected given the recent increases."

Why this is a legitimate case study, not cherry-picking: the model's own stated reasoning is appropriately hedged (starts from real feature deltas, then explicitly discounts its own confidence against a real historical base rate) — it landed at p=0.55, barely past the 0.5 threshold, which is exactly the calibrated behavior you'd want from a model that's right but not certain.

Myanmar, week of 2026-07-13 — 2-week horizon

p = 0.65

Actual outcome: escalation occurred two weeks later, as forecast. Track A, native GDELT features.

"The current state of BM suggests a moderate risk of material-conflict escalation in the following week. The increase in events and distinct actors mentioned, as well as the decrease in material-conflict share, are mixed signals that don't conclusively indicate an escalation. However, considering the historical analogs, two out of five similar weeks led to escalations (40%), which is a significant indicator. Furthermore, the average source tone has become more negative and the Mean Goldstein score has increased significantly, indicating growing tensions and hostility."

A real reasoning error worth flagging rather than hiding: the Goldstein scale runs from -10 (most conflictual) to +10 (most cooperative) — a rising Goldstein score is directionally a de-escalation signal, not the "growing tensions" the model's own text claims. The model got the right answer (correctly identified real escalation two weeks out) partly through reasoning that misreads one of its own input variables. That's a genuine, useful data point on why a probability output and a plausible-sounding reasoning trace are not the same kind of evidence — the number can be right even when part of the stated "why" isn't, and a system relying on the reasoning trace as an audit tool needs to know that.

Ethiopia, week of 2024-11-18 — pure UCDP track, 1-week horizon

p = 0.98

Actual outcome: escalation occurred. Track C — flagged: known feature-labeling bug in this specific run, disclosed rather than hidden.

"Based on the current state of Ethiopia, there are alarming indicators pointing towards a potential material-conflict escalation in the following week. The country has seen a significant increase in events last week (-9), with a high proportion of those being related to material conflicts (30%)... the mean Goldstein score has increased by 0.36... the average source tone has become increasingly negative (+36)..."
The real bug: icl_ollama_track.py's _reindex_for_icl function maps each track's real feature columns onto the ICL forecaster's fixed GDELT-shaped feature slots by position so the same model class runs across all three tracks unmodified. On Track C that means UCDP's real columns (event counts, fatality counts, dyad counts) get labeled in the prompt and the reasoning text using GDELT's vocabulary ("material-conflict share," "Goldstein score," "source tone") — vocabulary that doesn't literally apply to UCDP's schema at all. The probability (0.98) is real and was computed from real UCDP numbers, and the actual outcome (escalation) is real and correctly predicted — but the natural-language reasoning quoted above describes those real numbers using the wrong variable names, so it should not be read as an accurate explanation of what actually drove this specific prediction. Ranked #1 in tab 05 to fix.
Pattern across all three

What "weeks or months out" actually looked like when it worked

In every real case above, the model was not reacting to a single dramatic spike — it was reading a combination of a rising event count, a shift toward material (vs. verbal) conflict framing, and (correctly, in two of three cases) a Goldstein-scale move toward the conflictual end, then explicitly moderating its own confidence against how often similar historical configurations actually led to escalation. That "raise confidence from raw deltas, then discount against historical base rate" pattern is a real, if simple, form of reasoning a fixed-formula heuristic fallback could approximate but wouldn't spontaneously produce the appropriately hedged 0.55–0.65 probabilities for — none of these three real predictions were overconfident, which is itself notable given how easy overconfident false alarms are to produce from a small set of feature deltas.

Ranked, by expected impact

What to try next — every idea here judged free, safe, and possible

Ranked by a real, stated combination of (a) how directly this round's actual evidence points at it and (b) how cheap it would be to try. The separate, deliberately-not-pursued ideas (surveillance, dual-use, information-hazard risks) are in dangerous-ideas-log.html, not here.

  1. 1

    Fix the ICL reindexing bug (tab 04) so Track B/C real-LLM reasoning is semantically accurate

    Give each track its own native prompt template instead of positionally reindexing onto Track A's GDELT-shaped feature slots. Cheap (an afternoon, not a redesign), and directly fixes a disclosed accuracy problem in this round's own output rather than a hypothetical improvement.

    Why #1: it's the only item on this list that's fixing something this round's own evidence proved is currently wrong, not speculating about an upgrade.

  2. 2

    Move label spreading's decision threshold off the default 0.5

    Finding 3 (tab 03) showed 39.7% of label-spreading runs alert on literally nothing despite competitive AP. A precision-recall-curve-based threshold (or reporting only precision@top-N for this model family, which this project already computes every fold) would very likely turn a currently-unusable "wins on paper" model into a usable one.

    Why #2: directly addresses a concrete, quantified, currently-real failure mode with a same-code-path fix.

  3. 3

    Give the UCDP hypergraph its own real hyperedge type: shared conflict/dyad identity

    UCDP's conflict_name/dyad_name fields (already downloaded, not yet used as hyperedges) directly encode "these events are the same real conflict" — plausibly a stronger structural signal than the actor-code hyperedges that underperformed on this track (Finding 2). Worth a direct, disclosed ablation before concluding hypergraphs "don't help" on fatality-coded data generally.

  4. 4

    Stack, don't choose: blend the hypergraph model with GBM/ensemble per track

    Given Finding 2's clean track-dependent split, a simple learned blend (or even a fixed-weight average) of the hypergraph model with the tabular winner is a natural, cheap next real iteration this round didn't get to — ViEWS's own precedent (cited in the original literature review) is ensembling for genuine diversity, and these two families are about as diverse as this project's model battery gets.

  5. 5

    Extend Track B with the external features (climate, food price, structural, election) that only ever covered the original 6 countries

    Disclosed scope limitation from this round (tab 01) — Track B is GDELT-native features only. Given election-calendar proximity was this project's single strongest lever in an earlier round, re-testing it at 19-country/3-year scale is a real, concrete next step, not a vague "more data" gesture.

  6. 6

    Rebuild the GKG theme-embedding feature per-theme rather than per-week-averaged

    Finding 4's mildly negative result for gkg_embed is plausibly an aggregation artifact — averaging 12-dim embeddings across every theme active in a week may wash out a sparse but real signal from the specific 1–2 conflict-relevant themes. Keeping the top-k highest-weight themes' embeddings separately (rather than one blended average) is a concrete, cheap variant to test.

  7. 7

    Independent validation split: leave-one-country-out, not just rolling-origin-in-time

    Flagged as a real gap in the prior round's grand search and still true here — every result in this document tests generalization across time within known countries, never generalization to a country the model has never seen. A genuinely different, harder, and arguably more decision-relevant question for an early-warning system meant to cover new regions.

  8. 8

    Conformal prediction for real calibrated uncertainty intervals

    Distribution-free, doesn't require retraining the underlying models, and would give every prediction in this project an honest interval instead of a point probability — directly useful given how much of this document's own analysis (Findings 2 and 3 especially) is about probabilities that look confident but shouldn't be trusted at face value.

  9. 9

    Register for ACLED and add it as a fourth track

    Carried forward from every prior round of this project — still not done, still free for academic/nonprofit use, still the RFP's own stated evaluation ground truth. Ranked below the items above only because it requires an external registration step outside this session's control, not because the evidence for it is weaker.

  10. 10

    Extend the UCDP track past 2024 with the Candidate Events Dataset

    UCDP GED v25.1 ends 2024-12-31; UCDP's separately-published Candidate Events Dataset is more current but semi-automated/lower-curation. Worth adding as an explicitly lower-confidence extension of Track C, not blending into the main fatality-coded panel.
